# Distinctive assets — recognition before aesthetic certainty

## Source and attribution

**Documented** — Romaniuk's institute article describes distinctive assets using fame and uniqueness. The
OUP page identifies her 2018 book and its practitioner framework. Ward and colleagues' 2020 journal
article supplies a separate empirical comparison. [Institute
article](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/brands-of-distinction); [publisher
listing](https://www.oup.com.au/books/higher-education/business-marketing/9780190311506); [journal
preview](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6).

**Interpretation** — Evidence grade: useful empirical support for asset-type differences; practitioner
guidance for management decisions; no causal estimate of an identity engagement's sales effect.

## Business or creative problem

**Interpretation** — A designer can make an element look unusual without establishing that buyers link it
to the intended brand. Treat formal difference, familiarity and correct attribution as separate
questions.

## Research evidence

### Documented — practitioner account

**Documented** — The institute article says assets must evoke the intended brand and be widely linked to
it; new elements begin without strong fame or uniqueness. It recommends measuring remembered associations
rather than relying on the element's mere presence. [Institute
article](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/brands-of-distinction).

**Documented** — The book's public contents include mental availability and asset-strength measurement.
This task opened the listing, not the full book. [OUP
listing](https://www.oup.com.au/books/higher-education/business-marketing/9780190311506).

### Documented — empirical comparison

**Documented** — Ward and colleagues tested 1,281 in-market elements across 13 consumer packaged goods
categories and 19 countries. Their abstract reports stronger unique-ownership potential for characters,
logos and logotypes than colour, with variation within types. [Journal preview,
abstract](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6).

**Interpretation** — This is a comparison of existing assets, not random assignment of brands to identity
treatments. It cannot alone tell us what would happen if one particular client commissioned a new logo.

### Study design, sample and uncertainty

**Documented** — The preview identifies a Competitive Intensity measure. Its appendices describe
category-specific buyer screening and respondent counts. [Journal
preview](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41262-020-00187-6).

**Unknown** — We have not read the subscription-only full method or results. No pooled respondent count,
causal effect size, confidence interval or formula is reconstructed here. Asset count is not participant
count.

**Unknown** — No independent replication or meta-analysis was evaluated in this task. This is an evidence
limit, not a claim that replication does not exist.

## Evidence tiers for practice

**Interpretation** — The following proposed triage, adapted from Founding Blueprint v1.0, 22 October 2026
([founding source archive](../../studio/archive/founding-blueprint-v1.0.md)), complements the four evidence labels; it does not replace them or certify every claim
in a topic.

- **Documented** — **Research-supported**: use a cited study for the narrow relationship it actually
examines, with its design and limits. Here the asset-type comparison has empirical support; an identity's
sales impact does not follow from it.
- **Hypothesis** — **Context-dependent**: cultural symbolism, specific colour associations, luxury codes
and emotional readings need evidence from the actual audience and category. Test interpretations instead
of applying a universal lookup table.
- **Unknown** — **Reject-without-evidence**: universal colour-personality formulas, geometric-shape
persuasion promises and guaranteed conversion gains are not supported by the reviewed material. Do not
use them to justify a design.

## Category entry points and recognition

**Documented** — Romaniuk's April 2025 blog describes category entry points as situational cues that
prompt category-related brand memories and warns against confusing them with unique selling propositions.
It links to a longer article; only the blog was reviewed here.
[Blog](https://www.jenniromaniuk.com/blog/2025/4/8/category-entry-points-dissected-how-they-really-contribute-to-growth).

**Interpretation** — A recognisable asset answers which brand a cue identifies; a category-entry-point
enquiry asks in which buying situation a brand might be recalled. Keep these tasks separate instead of
calling a logo-recognition test a complete mental-availability study.

**Hypothesis** — Interview relevant buyers about actual occasions and motives, then build a provisional
situation-to-association worksheet. Do not turn an interview theme into a representative category
frequency or promise that adding more messages will cause growth.

## Strategic or conceptual insight

**Interpretation** — Ask which cue retrieves the brand, under which conditions, and with what competitor
confusion. An attractive symbol and an established distinctive asset are different stages of a design's
life.

**Interpretation** — Mental availability is broader than recognising one mark. A cue-identification task
alone should not be reported as a complete mental-availability assessment or prediction of market share.

## Visual and technical decisions

**Hypothesis** — Retain the brand name while building a new cue. A new business should not imitate mature
brands' ability to communicate anonymously without first testing recognition.

**Hypothesis** — Audit individual cues and combinations: colour alone, shape alone, wordmark and complete
application. Use equally legible stimuli, separate participants where learning would contaminate
comparisons, and record competitor answers.

## Why these decisions may work

**Interpretation** — Measuring attribution could expose a mismatch between the studio's sense of
originality and buyers' actual associations. It could also identify an apparently unfashionable cue that
deserves protection.

**Interpretation** — A small convenience sample can reveal confusion worth investigating; it cannot
establish population fame. Record who took part, prior familiarity and exposure conditions before
interpreting a percentage.

## Transferable principles

**Interpretation** — Use the [MASP study](../projects/porto-rocha-masp.md) to ask what deserves
retention, and the [Spotify study](../projects/collins-spotify.md) to ask whether a treatment groups
material. Neither portfolio demonstrates buyer memory on its own.

**Hypothesis** — Before discarding a client's legacy cue, compare its attribution with a proposed
replacement. Reject the replacement if it removes useful identification without a sufficient benefit on
the project's other criteria.

## What not to copy

**Interpretation** — Do not lift another brand's recognisable cue and expect its associations to transfer
beneficially. Competitor confusion is a risk to measure, not evidence of successful distinctiveness.

## Claims we must not make

- **Unknown** — We cannot claim that an unusual logo automatically becomes a distinctive asset.
- **Unknown** — We cannot claim a fixed sales uplift, premium-price effect or market-share gain from this
research.
- **Unknown** — We cannot claim that colour never works; an average comparison is not a universal
prohibition.
- **Unknown** — We cannot describe a publisher's contents page as proof that the entire book or its
underlying datasets were reviewed.
- **Unknown** — We cannot use the institute article's broad brain-activity language as a neuroscience
guarantee for a design.

## Evidence classification

### Documented

**Documented** — The journal abstract supports the stated asset counts and comparative finding. The
institute article and publisher listing serve different evidential roles and are not interchangeable with
the full empirical paper.

### Interpretation

**Interpretation** — Asset attribution is a useful design evaluation question; recognition is not
identical to liking, understanding or purchase.

### Hypothesis

**Hypothesis** — A measured cue audit could improve retention decisions relative to designer intuition
alone. Compare the decisions with later recognition evidence and revise the method if it fails to help.

### Unknown

**Unknown** — The reviewed materials do not establish the best asset for an emerging Indian consumer
business, a minimum viable sample, or recognition thresholds suitable for every category.

## Application to 3RDWRONG

**Hypothesis** — On a real founder-led consumer project, agree an exploratory cue test after establishing
the target category, relevant buyers and existing familiarity. Keep the outcome as diagnosis rather than
a scientific guarantee.

**Hypothesis** — Include required scripts and the actual selling context in stimuli. If recruitment is
limited, report the observations and limitations directly; do not dress a small studio exercise as a
nationally representative study.

## Open questions

- **Unknown** — Which of the client's existing cues have memory value among actual buyers?
- **Unknown** — How much do prior exposure and category familiarity explain any observed attribution
difference?
- **Hypothesis** — Would a second sample reproduce the direction of the first result? Seek replication
before making a consequential change on a small apparent difference.
