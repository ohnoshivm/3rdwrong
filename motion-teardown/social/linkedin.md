Most teams study award-level motion sites by eye. They copy the look and miss the mechanism.

On 30 October we tried the other way round. We took a 12-second screen recording of oryzo.ai, a product site by Lusion, and set out to learn exactly how its hero and first scroll section work. About six hours later we had a working rebuild in crimson and white, running locally.

The work was done by AI agents we directed with eleven written briefs. Three things made the difference:

1. Evidence in a fixed order. The video showed us what happened. The site's own code showed us how. When they disagreed, the code won. Our first guess, that the glowing screen edge was triggered by hovering over a 3D hand, was wrong: it is driven by scroll position, between 4,950 and 5,400 pixels.

2. Briefs written like good handover notes. Each one had an objective, a strict scope, the facts already known, a method, a list of files the agent owned, rules for tagging evidence, and the exact shape of the report we wanted back.

3. Parallel work with clear ownership. Three agents took the teardown apart by evidence type: code, live capture, and design tokens. Three more built the rebuild; the two that shared one page were joined by a single contract file, written before either started.

We have written the whole thing up as a long-form guide: the eight-stage method, every brief we sent, what Oryzo is doing under the surface (in our own words), the mistakes we made, and a worksheet for applying it to a site you admire. Full credit to Lusion, whose site was worth studying this closely.
