# Stillsoil Atelier fictional client brief

**Fictional example for testing methods and skills. Not a client.**

> Everything in this brief, including founders, products, interviews, dates,
> preferences and business details, is invented for method testing.

## Business and engagement context

- **Name:** Stillsoil Atelier (fictional; name check is documented in
  [examples index](../README.md)).
- **Business:** founder-led premium personal-care start-up, fictional base in
  Bengaluru, India.
- **Stage:** fictional early commercial traction through a small online shop
  and occasional in-person markets.
- **Product:** three cleansing bars and one body oil; one bar is fragrance-free.
  No product efficacy or clinical claims are supplied.
- **Decision-maker:** fictional founder code F1; the founder can approve strategy
  and visuals and owns the budget in this scenario.
- **Trigger:** a fictional retailer has asked for a concise line sheet and
  consistent product labels; the founder finds current touchpoints difficult
  to explain as one brand.
- **Requested work:** positioning clarification, identity direction and a
  system that can support agreed labels, a line sheet and an online shop.
- **Constraints:** no packaging engineering, full website redesign or ongoing
  social production is included in this sample scope.

## Fictional source notes

These notes exist only inside the scenario. They are not actual interviews.

- F1, founder interview: “I want the products to feel considered, not clinical.
  Please do not make the fragrance-free bar look like a medical product.”
- F1, founder interview: current labels use three unrelated typefaces and the
  online shop crops the product name on mobile.
- R1, fictional customer conversation: they buy the fragrance-free bar for
  personal preference and want the variant to be obvious before purchase.
- R2, fictional retailer conversation: they need product name and variant to
  scan quickly on a compact shelf card; they have not committed to stocking.
- R3, fictional customer conversation: they chose a bar after seeing a friend
  use it; they could not recall the current brand name a week later.
- R4, fictional customer conversation: they like tactile paper but do not want
  a label that makes use instructions hard to read.

No sample size or response represents real consumers. Do not infer a category
trend from these four fictional inputs.

## Desired change and success criteria

Within this fiction, the founder wants a more coherent identity that explains
the product line and can travel from a small label to a line sheet and online
shop. Success for the skills test means:

- Trace each material observation to a fictional input above.
- Separate founder preference from customer and retailer observations.
- Identify tensions and unknowns without inventing resolution.
- Produce positioning and creative criteria as hypotheses pending approval.
- Make no sales, efficacy, market or recognition promise.

## Open questions

- What evidence supports the word “premium” beyond the founder's intended
  segment?
- What product and print specifications will the eventual vendor require?
- Is the retailer request an opportunity, a hypothetical scenario detail, or
  only exploratory interest? No order is documented.
- Which languages and scripts need to appear on labels or commerce materials?
- Which claims, ingredients and regulatory statements are approved for use?

## Status and permissions

All material is synthetic. No person, company, product, quote or result is
real. This sample may be used only to test methods and skills; it is not a case
study or testimonial.

# 01 Discovery

## Objective

Establish what is known about the business, people, offer and operating context before interpreting the brief. Discovery records evidence and limits; it does not choose a visual route.

## Required inputs

- The client brief and the questions the engagement must answer.
- A commercial decision question and initial audience-evidence boundary, using the [perception-to-identity map](../perception-to-identity-map.md) where it helps frame the work.
- Available brand, product, sales-channel and communication materials with provenance.
- Access to relevant decision-makers and research participants, with consent and a note of limits.

## Activities

1. Read the brief once for the client’s stated need, then again for assumptions and missing information.
2. Inventory existing materials; record author, date, intended audience and permission to use.
3. Interview the decision-maker about the business trigger, offer, alternatives, constraints and definition of progress.
4. Where appropriate, speak with a small, varied set of audience or channel participants; ask for past behaviour and concrete examples, not only preferences.
5. Record observations close to source wording. Separate what was said from the team’s interpretation.
6. Log unanswered questions and access gaps; do not silently fill them with market stereotypes.

## Research methods

- Use semi-structured interviews with consent and consistent core prompts.
- Use contextual inquiry only when a real behaviour or artefact can be observed.
- Use desk research for category, competitor and cultural context; attach source links and access dates.

## Deliverables

- A source inventory and anonymised evidence notes.
- A concise research intake with participant roles, method, date and limitations.
- A list of confirmed constraints, contested claims and unknowns.
- A discovery synthesis with Documented, Interpretation, Hypothesis and Unknown labels, linked to the commercial problem and audience understanding.

## Quality criteria

- Every material claim points to a source or is labelled as a hypothesis.
- Founder preference is not presented as audience evidence.
- Participants are not treated as a representative sample without a suitable design.
- Sensitive data and unnecessary personal details are not copied into public files.
- The discovery questions relate to decisions the engagement can change.

## Common failure modes

- Interviewing only the founder and calling it consumer research.
- Treating compliments or stated preference as proof of purchase behaviour.
- Collecting references without checking provenance or rights.
- Using a demographic stereotype to fill an evidence gap.

## Human approval

Human reviewers approve the factual summary and participant handling. The client confirms the business facts and may correct them; the studio retains responsibility for evidence labels and does not imply approval of interpretations.

## Completion conditions

- Material inputs and access limits are documented.
- Open questions are prioritised by whether they could change the diagnosis or strategy.
- The discovery synthesis is ready for diagnosis; no visual direction is implied.

# Perception-to-identity map

This is a **proposed operating framework, not an academically validated
model**. It is a way to connect commercial questions to identity decisions;
it does not claim that a visual intervention causes a business result.
Framework source: *Founding Blueprint v1.0, 22 October 2026 ([founding source archive](../studio/archive/founding-blueprint-v1.0.md))*.
Psychology claims require their own cited research sources and context.

## Six-step path

| Step | Working question | Evidence and output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial problem | What business decision or friction makes identity work relevant now? | Document the trigger, business context, alternatives and boundary of the engagement. |
| Audience understanding | Who must notice, interpret or use the identity, and what do we know about their behaviour? | Record source, method, sample and limits; keep unknown audiences visible. |
| Strategic meaning | What should the brand mean in this context, and what proof or experience supports that meaning? | Define the strategic direction and distinguish intended meaning from observed response. |
| Creative expression | What original idea can make the intended meaning concrete? | Develop distinct concepts in words and rough sketches before styling or polishing. |
| Recognition and experience | What remains identifiable across the applications people encounter? | Test distinctive assets, hierarchy, language, use, accessibility and production. |
| Measurement and iteration | What can be checked now, and what requires later audience or business evidence? | Record test method, result, uncertainty, next action and the decision to retain, revise or drop. |

## Capability lenses and practical methods

These lenses describe questions to ask, not guaranteed effects.

| Capability | Practical method | Evidence limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attention and hierarchy | Inspect intended viewing distance, order of information, competing elements and first-read task; test with representative content. | A designer's inspection does not establish that an audience noticed or understood it. |
| Memory and distinctive assets | Inventory repeatable visual and verbal assets; compare their consistency and uniqueness against a sourced category sample. | Familiarity or recognition needs audience testing; don't infer memory from repetition in mock-ups. |
| Category entry points | Map occasions, needs or contexts that bring the audience into the category; ask about recent behaviour and compare messages across those contexts. | Small or convenience samples produce hypotheses, not category-wide claims. |
| Perceptual fluency | Check clarity, legibility, grouping and visual hierarchy under realistic conditions; compare alternatives without hiding complexity. | Ease of processing is not a proxy for preference, trust or purchase. Context and familiarity matter. |
| Semiotics and cultural codes | Identify the source and context of a sign, ask whose interpretation matters, and consult informed local reviewers where appropriate. | A designer's reading is an interpretation; do not generalise one person's reading to a culture. |
| Perceived quality and trust | Compare signals in material, finish, language, detail and consistency with the actual product and service experience. | Perceived quality is an audience response; a studio cannot claim it from art direction alone. |

## Small-sample rule

Five to eight interviews can generate useful hypotheses and reveal questions
worth testing. They do not support representative claims about a market or
audience. Report who was spoken to, how they were recruited, what was asked
and what was not covered. Do not count duplicate notes from one person as
independent agreement.

## Use in the engagement

- **Discovery (01):** define the commercial question and audience evidence
  boundary before gathering material.
- **Diagnosis (02):** connect evidence to the problem frame and distinguish
  symptoms from causes.
- **Strategy (03):** translate the diagnosis into intended meaning, criteria
  and proof boundaries; require human approval before concept work.
- **Validation (07):** test recognition, legibility, production and audience
  interpretation with methods appropriate to the claim.

See the [engagement map](identity-engagement/README.md) and
[3w-brand-discovery skill](../skills/3w-brand-discovery/SKILL.md). The blueprint
source is available in the [founding archive](../studio/archive/founding-blueprint-v1.0.md); later decisions supersede it where they differ.
