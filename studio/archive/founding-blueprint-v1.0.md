# 3RDWRONGS

The studio is now named 3RDWRONG ([D-0022](../decisions/0022-studio-name.md)); this archive keeps the original name verbatim.

This is the founding source document, partly AI-researched. It is retained in full as reference; later decision records supersede it wherever they differ. Publication was approved by the founder on 2026-10-22.

THE FOUNDING BLUEPRINT · V1.0 · 22 OCTOBER 2026

Strategy, brand intelligence, creative excellence, technology, business architecture, and a 90-day execution system.

## Part 1 — The strategic diagnosis

My central recommendation is to build 3RDWRONGS as an independent brand identity studio specialising in distinctive, culturally intelligent, living brand systems.

Begin with positioning, identity, typography, and art direction. Make motion and adaptive digital expression a signature capability. Build a proprietary process for connecting commercial strategy, human perception, and original visual expression.

Do not begin as a full-service agency. Do not sell AI as the primary reason to hire us. Do not make experimental complexity the standard output of every project.

The first difficult decision is accepting that a studio can have global ambitions without offering globally comprehensive services.

### The five realities we must confront

1\. We are building two businesses simultaneously.

One is a creative institution: a company capable of producing unusually good work.

The other is a commercial enterprise: a company capable of acquiring clients, managing money, delivering projects, and maintaining margins.

Both must progress. We cannot spend six months developing our taste without testing demand, and we cannot spend every week delivering inexpensive work without improving our craft.

2\. Access to elite methods does not confer elite ability.

Studying COLLINS's identity systems or Studio Dumbar's motion design provides strong reference points. It does not automatically provide their accumulated judgment, technical competence, creative relationships, or client trust.

What we can accelerate is the development of structured knowledge and practice. What we cannot skip is making work, having it criticised, and revising it.

3\. Taste-first is an internal standard, not sufficient market positioning.

Clients rarely initiate a project because they want a studio with taste. They are usually facing a concrete business problem: an indistinct brand, weak perceived value, inconsistent presentation, a repositioning, or a launch.

Our taste must produce a result they value.

4\. AI makes production capacity more accessible, raising the importance of judgment.

Our defensible advantage should not be generating more logos, concepts, or mockups.

It should be discovering better ideas, making better decisions, developing coherent systems, and delivering a better final result.

5\. The portfolio is our first product.

Without independently demonstrated creative capability, ambitious positioning becomes a claim rather than evidence.

We need serious portfolio work, but not at the expense of commercial validation.

### The strategic opportunity

My thesis is that many businesses will have access to increasingly inexpensive design production, while fewer will possess strong, coherent, recognisable identities.

That creates an opportunity for a small, technologically capable studio that combines:

Brand intelligence

Audience, category, culture, positioning, meaning

Creative authorship

Concepts, typography, visual expression, art direction

System engineering

Scalable identity components, motion, applications

Commercial thinking

Recognition, differentiation, desirability, effectiveness

This is an investment thesis for the company, not an established market forecast. We must validate whether prospective clients pay a premium for this combination.

## Part 2 — The design company landscape

There are several defensible models for an exceptional creative business, but they require different assets and capabilities.

| Model | Advantage | Main difficulty | Suitability now |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Strategic brand consultancy | Valuable executive-level decisions | Needs credibility, research, stakeholder management | Later |
| Independent identity studio | Focused craft, portfolio, clear deliverables | Crowded and subjective market | Highest |
| Motion-led identity studio | Memorable, expressive, digital-native work | Specialised craft and production | High, as differentiator |
| Experimental creative technology | Strong technical differentiation | Uncertain demand and expensive execution | Frontier |
| Consumer brand incubator | Opportunity for equity and owned IP | Capital, distribution, operational risk | Later |
| Multidisciplinary agency | Larger scopes and client relationships | High coordination costs, diluted standards | Avoid initially |

### Three viable strategic directions

A. The contemporary identity specialist

Build distinctive brands for ambitious consumer, lifestyle, cultural, and technology companies.

Strength: strong visual portfolio and a focused initial offer.

Risk: aesthetic differentiation alone is easy to imitate.

B. The living identity specialist

Create identity systems that work through typography, motion, behaviour, code, and interaction.

Strength: bridges identity design and creative engineering.

Risk: can overengineer simple client problems.

C. The perception-led brand consultancy

Combine positioning, cultural research, psychology, and a coherent identity to help companies become more recognisable and desirable.

Strength: closer connection to commercial decision-making.

Risk: difficult to establish strategic authority without a track record.

### Recommendation: combine A and B, supported by C

A brand identity studio first.

A living identity specialist by capability.

A perception-led consultancy by method.

This lets us develop valuable design fundamentals before competing in expensive experiential production or broad organisational strategy.

Our narrowest initial proposition could be:

> 3RDWRONGS builds distinctive brand identities for ambitious businesses that need to look, sound, and behave like something unmistakably their own.

That is a working proposition, not yet a finished brand manifesto.

## Part 3 — Positioning, philosophy, and offer

### Initial market

Prioritise founder-led businesses with an actual commercial reason to commission branding.

My proposed first testing segments are:

1. Design-conscious consumer and lifestyle brands: fashion, personal care, homeware, speciality food, and premium products.
2. Emerging technology and creative software companies: products needing distinctiveness in visually similar categories.
3. Cultural and independent hospitality businesses: businesses where atmosphere, personality, and artistic expression matter.

I would choose consumer and lifestyle brands as our primary creative territory, with selective technology projects to demonstrate adaptive systems.

These are hypotheses, not proven client segments. The first 20 buyer conversations should determine which segment is commercially strongest.

### Initial offer

CORE SERVICE

#### The 3RDWRONGS Identity System

A focused strategic and creative engagement that takes a brand from unclear positioning to a distinctive, deployable identity.

Standard scope

- Brand discovery, competitor analysis, audience research, and positioning
- One coherent strategic direction
- Identity concept development and design exploration
- Core visual system: logo, typography, colour, composition, image direction
- Verbal identity foundations
- Key applications and motion principles
- Brand guidelines, asset library, and handover

Explicit exclusions: comprehensive websites, continuous social media production, advertising management, packaging engineering, major campaigns, and complex 3D experiences. These are separately scoped extensions or partner services.

### Creative philosophy: the working doctrine

3RDWRONGS needs a philosophy that guides decisions rather than merely sounding interesting.

I propose five principles.

Meaning before appearance. A creative concept must express something valuable about the brand, audience, or cultural context.

Distinctiveness before fashionable conformity. We should study category conventions and selectively break them when that creates an advantage.

Systems before isolated artefacts. A logo is one asset. An identity is a language that can produce many coherent expressions.

Expression with restraint. The right answer may be striking, subtle, restrained, strange, or deliberately ordinary. We should not force a signature aesthetic onto every client.

Originality through informed experimentation. Research establishes the foundations; new creative ideas must emerge from exploration, testing, and judgment.

### What the name 3RDWRONGS should mean

The name suggests unconventional thinking, willingness to challenge accepted answers, and a belief that meaningful originality can emerge through imperfect attempts.

The interpretation worth developing is not being wrong for attention.

It is refusing the obvious answer when a better one remains undiscovered.

That could become a productive creative philosophy. It is not yet a tested positioning, and we must check the name for trademark, domain, and pronunciation issues before investing heavily in it.

## Part 4 — Elite agency intelligence report

The most useful reference studios are not necessarily the most famous. I prioritised evidence of transferable creative methods, original case studies, and documented decision-making.

### The eight studios we should study first

1\. Experimental Jetset — Systems and creative philosophy

Foundational · Highest research priority

[The Whitney Tapes](https://www.jetset.nl/archive/the-whitney-tapes) is unusually valuable because the designers discuss discarded concepts, client dialogue, operational constraints, and why their eventual system emerged. Study how to create flexibility without losing identity.

2\. PORTO ROCHA — Cultural intelligence and identity

Foundational

[MASP identity](https://www.portorocha.com/masp) demonstrates how architecture, cultural history, typography, motion, and verbal identity can combine into a new system without discarding institutional memory.

3\. COLLINS — Brand platforms and expressive range

Foundational

[Spotify](https://wearecollins.com/case-studies/spotify/) and [Mailchimp](https://wearecollins.com/case-studies/mailchimp/) show how recognisable graphic assets, expressive variation, and a commercially relevant positioning can coexist.

4\. Studio Dumbar — Motion-native identity systems

High leverage

[DEMO 2025](https://studiodumbar.com/work/demo-2025) integrates typography, motion, sound, digital environments, and an identity that can evolve while retaining recognisable elements.

5\. DIA Studio — Generative identity

High leverage · Frontier capability

[Adidas London](https://dia.tv/project/adidas-flagship-london/) demonstrates programmable variation in brand content. It credits FIELD for its generative content-management component, an important reminder that remarkable work can depend on specialist collaboration.

6\. Pentagram — Typographic authorship and longevity

Foundational

[The Public Theater](https://www.commarts.com/exhibit/public-theater-identity) shows how a flexible, strongly identifiable typographic language can sustain repeated reinvention.

7\. DesignStudio (now Further) — Strategy into expression

Foundational

[Airbnb](https://www.design.studio/work/air-bnb) details the journey from immersion and customer understanding to the Belong Anywhere platform and a coherent identity. The case illustrates how strategy can govern more than marketing graphics.

8\. Koto — Identity consistency at scale

Later · Useful systems benchmark

[Amazon and Instagram work](https://koto.studio/) provides an instructive contrast to small identity projects: its Amazon work involved 15 markets and more than 50 sub-brands. We should learn from its architecture, not imitate the scale of its production organisation.

Other studios worth retaining on the longer research watchlist are BUCK, &Walsh, Wolff Olins, Base Design, Mucho, ManvsMachine, Instrument, and Universal Favourite. They are not immediate priorities because a larger catalogue is less useful than deeply understanding the first eight.

### Three projects to reverse-engineer rigorously

#### Case study A: Whitney Museum — Experimental Jetset

8

The documented problem: The museum was evolving and preparing for a move. It needed an identity that could accommodate changing exhibitions and be applied by its internal design team.

The documented process: Experimental Jetset's own account describes a project developed through extensive dialogue between 2011 and 2013. Early proposals included typographic approaches, multi-image compositions, and a comparatively static mark. The eventually selected responsive W emerged through refinement of the brief and design explorations. The studio says the work ultimately included templates and a 220-page identity manual.

The important design decision: Use a small, recognisable identity element whose form can respond to different compositions.

That makes the identity an adaptable visual relationship, not just a fixed symbol.

Our transferable method:

- Identify the primary tension in the brief: consistency versus expressive freedom.
- Explore several structural solutions before choosing a visual style.
- Define invariant elements and variable elements.
- Design for the people who will use the system after delivery.
- Evaluate whether the system remains recognisable when the applications change.

3RDWRONGS exercise: Design an identity for a fictional contemporary art institution using only one typographic principle and two graphic primitives. Produce 30 distinct applications that still feel like the same institution.

Do not reproduce the Whitney's W or its composition rules. Create a new formal grammar.

#### Case study B: MASP — PORTO ROCHA

11

The documented problem: The museum needed an identity suitable for its architectural expansion and future international ambitions without severing its Brazilian modernist roots.

The documented decisions: PORTO ROCHA evolved the wordmark using rectilinear forms, introduced a modular layout grid derived from the architecture, used the relationship between two museum buildings as a graphic cue, and developed motion behaviour inspired by the museum's glass-easel display system. Its work also extended into verbal expression and digital experience.

This is a powerful lesson in cultural intelligence.

The designers were not merely searching for an aesthetic associated with modernism. They used the institution's history, architecture, and cultural function as material for a contemporary design system.

Our transferable method:

1. Identify what the existing brand must preserve.
2. Research its physical, historical, and cultural environment.
3. Extract formal properties from that environment.
4. Develop a visual grammar rather than superficial references.
5. Connect the visual system to real applications and contemporary needs.

3RDWRONGS exercise: Reposition a hypothetical independent cultural institution in India using its actual regional design history as research material. Deliver an original system suitable for both local and international audiences.

#### Case study C: DEMO — Studio Dumbar

8

The original DEMO identity used variable typography and a custom script to create a distinctive system of animated transformations. Later editions expanded the language across larger physical environments, international screen networks, and digital worlds, while retaining core elements.

The transferable insight is that motion can be a rule of identity, not merely an animation applied to a finished logo.

Our exercise: define a kinetic brand using five motion principles. Test how its timing, transformation, and spatial behaviour express the intended personality.

Do not begin with impressive animation effects. Begin with what motion should communicate.

### What these studios actually teach us

Across these documented cases, I identify four recurring ideas:

- Strong identities generally have a coherent underlying concept.
- Variation becomes more useful when governed by a recognisable system.
- Contextual research can lead to more distinctive design decisions than trend references alone.
- Implementation requirements influence the creative solution, sometimes fundamentally.

Those are transferable observations. They are not claims that every studio uses the same methodology.

We cannot verify the firms' internal pricing, profitability, staffing ratios, detailed production pipelines, or rejected pitches from public case studies. Their websites are also promotional materials. Apparent commercial success following a rebrand should not be interpreted as proof that the rebrand caused that success.

## Part 6 — The psychology and brand perception system

We should establish a small research discipline before attempting to build a sophisticated psychological model of branding.

### Six essential capabilities

| Knowledge | What it changes in our work | Practical method |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Attention and visual hierarchy | Determines what viewers notice first | Five-second exposure followed by unaided recall |
| Memory and distinctive assets | Makes brands easier to recognise | Unbranded asset recognition tests |
| Category entry points | Connects brands with buying situations | Interview customers about purchase occasions |
| Perceptual fluency | Helps explain ease of interpretation | Compare comprehension across design directions |
| Semiotics and cultural codes | Connects visual choices to cultural meaning | Map category codes and test audience interpretations |
| Perceived quality and trust | Evaluates whether expression supports intended credibility | Compare designs in relevant purchase contexts |

The Ehrenberg-Bass work on distinctive assets and category entry points is particularly relevant. It connects branding to recognition and buying-situation memory rather than treating design as an isolated aesthetic activity.

### Our first proprietary framework: the Perception-to-Identity Map

1\. Commercial problem

What perception or behaviour needs to change?

2\. Audience understanding

What do people believe, recognise, expect, and value?

3\. Strategic meaning

What associations should the brand build?

4\. Creative expression

Which symbols, typography, colours, language, and behaviours express that meaning?

5\. Recognition and experience

Can the audience identify, understand, and use the system?

6\. Measurement and iteration

What changed, what did not, and what remains uncertain?

This is our proposed operating framework, not an academically validated model.

For an early consumer-brand project, we might interview five to eight people as a low-cost exploratory exercise, compare the resulting themes with category research, and use simple recognition and comprehension checks to identify obvious weaknesses.

These small samples are useful for generating hypotheses. They do not establish representative consumer preferences or statistically defensible predictions.

### Separate strong evidence from branding folklore

Useful, research-supported directions: recognisable identity cues, audience-context effects, visual hierarchy, category associations, and the difference between perceived and actual usability.

Useful but context-dependent: the emotional meaning of specific visual forms, luxury codes, cultural symbolism, colour associations, and narrative devices.

Claims to reject without suitable evidence: universal personality formulas based on colours, reliable psychological persuasion based on geometric shapes alone, guaranteed conversion improvements from visual changes, or claims that specific aesthetic styles trigger predictable subconscious behaviour.

For example, evidence on the aesthetic-usability effect supports a relationship between appearance and perceived usability. It does not prove that making an interface more attractive will necessarily make it function better.

Our objective should be to become unusually good at forming psychological hypotheses, expressing them through design, and testing them with appropriate evidence.

### Four particularly useful technical capabilities

1\. Identity-as-code

Use p5.js to create visual systems from explicit rules.

For example, a brand might have a core geometric primitive whose arrangement changes in response to content while retaining recognisable properties.

The real asset is not the generated image. It is the system that can produce many coherent expressions.

2\. Design tokens

Define a brand's digital components as structured values:

```text
{
  "brand": {
    "color": {
      "primary": "#D91D37",
      "surface": "#F4F1EB"
    },
    "type": {
      "display": "LicensedDisplay",
      "body": "LicensedBody"
    },
    "motion": {
      "fast": "180ms",
      "standard": "320ms"
    }
  }
}
```

These are illustrative values, not proposed colours or fonts for 3RDWRONGS.

When a project warrants it, Style Dictionary can transform tokens into formats used by websites and applications.

3\. Automated digital QA

Use [Playwright](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright) for browser screenshots, responsive verification, and repeatable testing.

Use [axe-core](https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core) for automated accessibility checks.

Do not confuse a passing automated test with complete accessibility compliance. Manual inspection and user testing remain necessary.

4\. AI-assisted creative investigation

Use AI to increase the breadth of options explored and the depth of research, rather than to dictate final aesthetic decisions.

A practical division of responsibility:

| Stage | AI responsibilities | Human responsibilities |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Research | Collect, classify, compare, surface contradictions | Verify evidence and determine relevance |
| Strategy | Generate hypotheses, objections, alternative positions | Select the commercial and creative direction |
| Concepts | Expand territories, investigate metaphors and references | Judge originality and conceptual strength |
| Production | Assist with variations, implementation, scripts, technical checks | Control typography, composition, detail, and authorship |
| Critique | Identify inconsistencies and foreseeable weaknesses | Decide what is genuinely better |
| Delivery | Prepare documentation, organise exports, run checks | Approve accuracy, licensing, quality, and completeness |

We should reuse the existing multi-model AI environment rather than build another elaborate routing system specifically for 3RDWRONGS.

### What we should not install immediately

Avoid building a large collection of AI-agent skills and MCP integrations before we have repeatable projects.

In particular, defer expensive image-generation pipelines, autonomous design agents, multiple overlapping design applications, self-hosted workflow orchestrators, custom 3D rendering infrastructure, and complex browser automation.

These become worthwhile when recurring work demonstrates a clear benefit.

### The five AI skills worth developing ourselves

Instead of searching for dozens of loosely maintained prompts, write five tested internal skills:

| Skill | Input | Output |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| `3w-brand-discovery` | Client brief, interviews, market data | Evidence-backed discovery dossier |
| `3w-category-code-audit` | Competitor references | Category conventions and opportunities |
| `3w-identity-system-critic` | Concepts and applications | Specific, argued creative critique |
| `3w-brand-consistency-qa` | Finished assets and rules | Inconsistency and implementation report |
| `case-study-architect` | Process records and outcomes | Accurate, evidence-led case study |

Each skill should have a fixed purpose, clear input requirements, example outputs, failure conditions, and human approval points.

Build them as Markdown instructions first. Add code only when it makes the output better or substantially reduces repeated work.

### C. The production workflow

1. Discover

   Establish commercial objectives, constraints, audiences, stakeholders, research needs, and success criteria.
2. Diagnose

   Analyse category conventions, brand associations, competitive positioning, customer perceptions, and existing equity.
3. Define

   Agree on a strategic position, creative brief, audience priority, and design problem.
4. Explore

   Develop different conceptual territories, not merely multiple stylistic versions of the same idea.
5. Design

   Build an identity's typography, forms, imagery, verbal expression, colour, and compositional behaviour.
6. Systematise

   Define invariants, variables, applications, digital rules, motion principles, and exceptions.
7. Validate

   Test strategic fit, recognition, legibility, production feasibility, and intended audience interpretation.
8. Deliver and learn

   Produce usable assets, documentation, client training, and a retrospective that improves the next project.

### D. The creative review system

We should use multiple modes of critique rather than relying on a single aesthetic opinion.

Concept review

Is there a compelling idea? Why is it relevant? Is it distinct from the category? Would it remain interesting without polished mockups?

Formal review

Does the hierarchy work? Are spacing, typography, proportion, colour, and composition controlled? Where does the system become inconsistent?

Audience review

What do people actually notice, understand, remember, and associate with the brand? Which conclusions depend only on designer assumptions?

Implementation review

Does it survive small formats, poor screens, print constraints, localisation, unusual content, and real operational use?

Do not average these into a fictional overall taste score.

Use written judgments:

- Strong enough to advance.
- Promising but structurally unresolved.
- Technically sound but conceptually weak.
- Distinctive but commercially inappropriate.
- Rejected, with explicit reasons.

The critique must end with the next design action, not an abstract assessment.

### E. The taste-development practice

A disciplined weekly practice would allocate:

| Activity | Weekly effort | Evidence of improvement |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Deeply analyse two reference projects | 3h | Original research cards |
| Typography and composition studies | 4h | Deliberate improvements across iterations |
| One original concept challenge | 4h | Unrelated creative applications |

Total: approximately 15 hours a week.

The key learning loop is:

Observe → Explain → Reconstruct principles → Apply → Critique → Revise.

This is how we attempt to turn individual taste into an organisational capability.

## Part 11 — Critical founder's review

These are the weaknesses I would challenge most aggressively.

### 1. We may be overestimating the value of research

Studying the best studios can become an intellectually respectable form of procrastination.

A person who has analysed 100 identities but cannot produce one excellent identity has not built a competitive capability.

I would impose a rule: substantial research must produce an original exercise or project application.

### 2. We may be confusing technological ability with creative originality

Code, AI, generative systems, and motion can make mediocre ideas look complicated.

Our default creative sequence should be concept first, coherent static identity second, and advanced expression third.

For some brands, excellent typography and photography may outperform an elaborate interactive system.

### 3. Our taste may not yet justify our positioning

We cannot appoint ourselves an exceptional creative studio through language.

Our initial standards must be proven by work, external critique, practical deployment, and increasingly demanding clients.

Founders should be able to identify where their own design work is weak without confusing personal attachment with quality.

### 4. We may be targeting clients who admire design but cannot pay for it

Aesthetic sophistication is not evidence of commercial readiness.

An emerging fashion label might love an identity system but have no meaningful budget. A less glamorous established company may have a clear commercial reason to pay for rebranding.

The market must be selected partly by buying behaviour, not purely by the kinds of projects we want in our portfolio.

### 5. We may be underestimating typography

Typography is one of the most important areas where AI-generated design can appear superficially convincing while lacking craft.

Kerning, optical balance, type selection, multilingual support, spacing, hierarchy, typographic voice, and their relationship to a concept require disciplined practice.

I would prioritise typographic competence above learning advanced 3D.

### 6. We may be defining differentiation too broadly

Taste-first, AI-first, technology-first, research-first, psychology-first, and strategy-first are too many competing claims for an initial market position.

Internally, all can inform our methods.

Externally, the proposition must be much clearer.

We create distinctive brand identities. We are especially good at turning them into coherent, expressive systems.

Everything else supports that promise.

### 7. We may be overlooking the importance of experienced collaborators

Some skills are difficult to accelerate without direct feedback.

High-level identity designers, typographers, motion specialists, and creative directors can see flaws that a new founder may not yet recognise.

A carefully chosen paid critique from an experienced professional could be more valuable than another collection of AI tools.

### 8. We may be attempting to develop too much proprietary IP too early

A new studio rarely needs its own design editor, asset-management system, or autonomous creative engine.

It needs an excellent working method and repeatable evidence of quality.

Build proprietary software once repeated client work reveals a specific opportunity that existing tools fail to address.

### 9. Our creative voice could become derivative

Constant exposure to the same famous studios risks reproducing the same visual vocabulary: oversized grotesk typography, saturated colour, algorithmic graphics, and contemporary editorial layouts.

We must expand our references beyond professional design.

Study architecture, literature, cinema, local art, objects, industrial design, scientific visualisation, music, and cultural history.

The purpose is not novelty for its own sake. It is developing a broader source of ideas than our competitors.

### 10. We may be too attached to the agency model itself

There is a credible alternative: build 3RDWRONGS initially as a founder-led independent creative practice rather than a conventional agency.

A small, selective studio with excellent contractors may achieve better work and healthier economics than a larger organisation with constant overhead.

We should not hire permanent staff or expand services until the demand and management capacity justify it.
